Put ten brands' AI-assisted blog posts side by side and cover the logos. Try to match post to brand. You can't. That's not a coincidence and it's not a prompt problem. It's a property of the technology, and it deserves a name.

**Regression to the template:** the tendency of AI-assisted content to drift toward the statistical average of everything ever published in its category, unless an editorial force pulls it back.

## Why it happens

A language model is trained to predict the most probable next word. Probable means common. Common means average. When you ask a model to "write a post about portfolio diversification," you are asking it to compute the centre of gravity of every diversification post ever written, most of which were mediocre. The model isn't failing when it produces beige. It is succeeding at exactly what it was built to do.

This is why the output always feels familiar. It is familiar. It's the mean.

## The symptoms

You already know them. Every paragraph opens with a question you didn't ask. Every list has three items. Every insight is hedged into mush. The vocabulary converges: unlock, seamless, elevate, dive in. Your fintech blog reads like your competitor's fintech blog, which reads like a car insurance blog, because at the level of statistics they are the same blog.

The commercial damage is quiet. Nothing is wrong enough to flag in review. It's just that nobody finishes reading, nobody remembers who said it, and nobody moves.

## Why better prompts don't fix it

Prompts nudge. Gravity wins. A clever system prompt shifts the first draft a few degrees off the mean, but every regeneration, every edit pass, every "make it punchier" drags the text back toward the centre. Volume makes it worse: publish fifty AI-assisted pieces a month with no counterweight, and your brand voice is now a rounding error on the training data.

Teams keep buying better tools to solve this. The tool is not the variable. The standard is.

## The counterweight

The only force that beats regression to the template is an editorial standard with teeth. Not a tone-of-voice PDF nobody opens. A working rubric that every draft passes or fails, whether a person wrote it or a model did:

1. **The cover test.** Cover the logo. Could a competitor publish this unchanged? If yes, it's template. Kill it or rewrite it.
2. **The banned list.** Every phrase your category overuses, written down and enforced. The list grows monthly, because the mean moves.
3. **The claim audit.** Every draft must contain at least one thing only you can say: a number you own, a decision you made, a mistake you paid for. The model can't supply this. That's the point.
4. **One bar, two writers.** Human drafts and AI drafts pass the same rubric. The moment AI output gets a lower bar, the drift begins.

I ran this as a working system across content in 150+ markets, in a regulated category where "sounding like everyone" also meant compliance risk. Output doubled. The voice held. Not because the models were special, but because the bar was.

The brands that survive the AI content flood will not be the ones that generate the most. They'll be the ones with the strongest pull against the mean. Gravity is undefeated only when nobody pushes back.
