Half of B2B buyers now start vendor research inside an AI chatbot, not a search box. By the time a human reads your content, a machine has already read it, judged it, and decided whether you exist in the answer. That means every piece you publish has two readers, and they want different things.

**The machine reader** decides whether to cite you. It rewards structure: direct answers stated early, claims tied to entities, schema that says what a page is, headings that mean what they say. It has no patience for throat-clearing and no ability to be charmed.

**The human reader** decides whether to trust you. They usually arrive after the machine has answered their first question, which means they come with the basics already handled and one thing left to settle: who actually knows this subject, and who is just summarising it? They reward judgment, lived detail, and a voice they can pick out of a lineup.

Most content fails both readers at once, because it's written for a third reader who no longer exists: the 2019 googler who clicked the third blue link and skimmed. Content for that ghost is too padded for the machine to cite cleanly and too generic for the human to remember.

## The model

Score every piece twice, once per reader.

**The citation test:** can a machine lift a clean, self-contained answer from this piece? Is the claim stated plainly enough to quote, structured enough to parse, specific enough to attribute?

**The byline test:** would a human who read this remember who wrote it a week later? Is there a judgment call, a number you own, a sentence nobody else would risk?

Two tests, four outcomes:

1. **Cited and remembered.** The machine quotes you; the human seeks you out. This is the whole game.
2. **Cited but forgotten.** You educated the market and someone else closed the deal. Commodity knowledge, cleanly structured. Better than invisible, but you're doing free labour for your category.
3. **Remembered but never cited.** A strong voice the machines can't parse. You'll keep your fans and lose every buyer who starts in a chatbot, which is now most of them.
4. **Neither.** Slop. The majority of what's published today, at industrial volume.

## How to write for both without pleasing neither

The mistake is averaging: content that is half-structured and half-voiced, which both readers score as noise. The two readers don't want a compromise. They want different layers of the same piece.

Structure is for the machine: the answer up top, stated as a claim, marked up so a parser knows what it found. Voice is for the human: the reasoning underneath, the experience that earned the claim, the sentence with a pulse. One piece, two layers, no mush.

I rebuilt a trading platform's search architecture around this before AI Overviews made it urgent: schema, pillar pages, intent clusters for the machine; editorial standards for the human. The brand stayed cited while category traffic fell double digits. Not because we chose the machine over the reader, but because we stopped pretending they were the same reader.

Your next content review needs exactly two questions. Can the machine quote it? Will the human remember you? Everything that fails both is volume you can stop producing today.
