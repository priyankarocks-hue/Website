In 2004, my job was to fill a business page by 6pm, every day, whether or not anything
interesting had happened that day. No AI, no content calendar software, just a reporter's
notebook and a deadline that did not move. Twenty years later, I run content and comms
functions where AI can draft in seconds what used to take my team a week. The tools changed
completely. What makes the output worth reading did not change at all.

I've spent this career on both sides of that line — as a journalist authoring columns and
running desks at two Indian newspapers, and later as the person who built a communications
function from scratch for a company that's now a top-3 global lab-grown diamond producer. Along
the way I've learned what actually transfers when the tools get radically faster. Here's what
held up, and what's new.

## 1. A deadline is a filter, not a punishment

Every reporter learns this the hard way: constraints don't hurt your work, they force the
decision about what actually matters. When I built our resource library at IIa Technologies —
a shared system of partner marketing kits, sales training, and campaign frameworks — the point
wasn't to produce more material. It was to cut our delivery time in half so the team spent its
remaining hours on judgment calls, not repetition. AI can now compress the "produce more"
problem to nearly zero. That just means the filter — what's actually worth publishing — is now
the entire job, not a side task.

## 2. The story still has to be verifiable

A newsroom trains a specific reflex: before it runs, you check it. Sources, numbers, quotes —
verified, or it doesn't print. That instinct is the single most useful thing I carry into AI
content operations today. AI-assisted content fails the same way an unverified story fails —
quietly, and usually at the worst possible moment, in front of the audience you were trying to
earn. The teams who get real value from AI content are the ones who kept an editor's skepticism
in the loop, not the ones who removed it to move faster.

## 3. Distribution was always the other half of the job

Writing a good column never mattered if nobody found it. The muscle I built pitching editors
and placing stories in Bloomberg, CNBC, and Discovery is the same muscle that later got our
email campaigns to 70%+ engagement and 13–20% conversion on a premium product line. AI has made
content production nearly free. It has not made attention free. If anything, distribution and
media relations — genuinely earning a placement, a share, an inbox open — are worth more now
than they were when production was the bottleneck.

## 4. Voice is the thing you can't outsource, to a junior or to a model

I managed junior writers and freelancers across cities for years before I ever managed an AI
system, and the failure mode is identical: without a real editorial standard, output drifts
generic within weeks. The fix was never "hire better" or "prompt better" — it was building a
rubric, applying it consistently, and catching drift early. That's exactly the system I now
build for clients scaling AI content: a voice standard strict enough that a reader can't tell
which piece came from a human draft and which came from an AI-assisted one, because both were
held to the same bar.

## 5. The best career advice is the same advice, again

Twenty years ago, someone told me the only way to get better at writing was to write on
deadline, in public, where it could be judged. That's still true. AI didn't change what makes
content good — clarity, verification, a real point of view, and distribution that respects your
audience's attention. It changed how fast you can find out whether you're doing those four
things well. That's actually the opportunity: the feedback loop got shorter. Use it.

---

None of this is nostalgia for the newsroom. It's the opposite — the skills a newsroom forces on
you turn out to be exactly the skills an AI-augmented content operation needs most, and most
teams building with AI right now have never had to develop them.
